\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The measured matrix shows substantial task-dependent variation among the evaluated implementations. The classifier's macro-F1 is high on Banking77, CLINC150 plus, SMS Spam, and LEDGAR, and much lower on PubMedQA, VitaminC, and ESCI. Laya exceeds the classifier on PubMedQA and on Civil Comments under macro-F1, and is far from it on Banking77 and LEDGAR. On SMS Spam, the rule reaches macro-F1 0.8576 compared with 0.9393 for the classifier; this is the only task on which a rule is evaluated. Qwen has the lowest measured macro-F1 among eligible primitives on seven of the eight tasks. On VitaminC it is 0.2209 and Laya is 0.2165. The source of the performance gap differs across tasks. When \(V\) is near zero, the remaining gap primarily reflects errors among legal labels; when \(V\) is near one, output-contract failure dominates.

The frozen predictor specification does not allow Qwen to have a primitive-specific fingerprint slope. Under that restriction, the primitive-mean baseline is strong because it already carries the level of each primitive. The fingerprint sometimes moves macro-F1 error down. It usually moves invalid-output-rate error up, including on the tasks where the contract failure is largest. Even the helpful ESCI movement does not change the policy. LLMRouterBench reports a related pattern inside LLM routing: a method can fail to outperform a simple baseline even when model behavior is heterogeneous (Li, Zhang, et al.,~\citeyear{li2026llmrouterbench}). We cite that as a caution, not as the same experiment.

The study does not establish that a fingerprint with primitive-specific slopes, a larger task sample, or a different generative configuration would fail. It also does not establish that such a specification would succeed. The protocol was frozen before \cref{tab:selector} existed. Changing the model after observing the prediction errors would compromise that pre-specification.
